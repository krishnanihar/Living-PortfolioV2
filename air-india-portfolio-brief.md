## Full Narrative (Long-form Case Study)

### Context

In January 2022, Tata Group acquired Air India after 69 years of government ownership. They inherited 140 legacy IT systems, four airlines to merge, decades of technical debt, and no design infrastructure—no system, no tokens, no documentation, no shared language between design and engineering.

I joined DesignLAB in the middle of this $200M transformation. My role wasn't to maintain what existed. It was to build what didn't.

Two years later, the work our team shipped won a Red Dot Design Award, Gold and Bronze Stevie Awards, Good Design Award Australia, APEX Four Star Global Airline recognition, and the Opus Research Conversational AI Award for the industry's first generative AI chatbot. The mobile app we redesigned from scratch hit a 4.7 App Store rating—the highest of any Indian airline.

---

### The Foundation Problem

When there's no design system, every screen is an island. Designers were making isolated decisions. Engineers were interpreting specs differently. Reviews caught inconsistencies too late—if at all. The speed Tata demanded was impossible without infrastructure.

I started by reverse-engineering what existed. Hundreds of screens, undocumented, built over years by people who'd since left. I extracted the implicit logic—spacing patterns, typography decisions, color usage—and codified it into a systematic token framework. Variables, naming conventions, hierarchy. The foundation that would let four merging airlines eventually speak the same design language.

But documentation alone doesn't scale. Teams needed tooling.

So I built Pixel Radar—a Figma plugin that automates consistency checks. What started as a personal workflow fix became infrastructure serving 450+ daily users, cutting design review time by 30%. It's still running. It solved a problem the organization didn't have budget or bandwidth to address through official channels.

---

### The Handoff Problem

Design systems only matter if engineering implements them accurately. Handoff at Air India was fragmented—Figma links in Slack threads, specs that didn't match builds, endless back-and-forth.

I implemented a design-dev handoff workflow using Model Context Protocol—bridging design and engineering through AI-assisted tooling. Structured, reliable, modern. The kind of workflow a transformed airline should have, not the duct-tape process we inherited.

---

### The Product Problem

While rebuilding infrastructure, I was simultaneously shipping features. The IFE (in-flight entertainment) system needed interfaces designed for constraints most designers never think about—typography legible at seat-back distance, touch targets that work during turbulence, content hierarchy for passengers browsing at 35,000 feet, accessibility spanning toddlers to elderly travelers with zero onboarding. This work contributed to Air India winning Asia's Leading Airline Inflight Entertainment at the World Travel Awards.

I also redesigned the NPS feedback system—auditing existing flows, identifying friction through data, and rebuilding how Air India listens to its passengers. Feedback went from a checkbox to a strategic input.

And I developed a competitor analysis framework—a research methodology synthesizing patterns across 15+ airline and travel apps, giving the team an ongoing reference for what world-class looks like.

---

### The Future Problem

Transformation isn't just catching up. It's leapfrogging.

While the organization modernized basics, I designed Search with AI—an AI-native search experience using natural language understanding, rethinking how passengers interact with an airline. I presented it at Battle of Apps. This was part of a broader push that led to Air India's generative AI booking feature winning the Red Dot Design Award 2024—now showcased in the Red Dot Design Museum in Singapore—and the airline deploying the industry's first generative AI virtual assistant, AI.g, which won the Opus Research Conversational AI Award.

---

### The Team Problem

You can't transform products without transforming the people building them. Tata inherited employees from legacy Air India—talented individuals, but no shared culture, no common design vocabulary, no upskilling infrastructure.

I initiated Liftoff and other team programs—workshops, skill shares, critique rituals—building the collaborative culture a transformation of this scale demands. I didn't wait for HR. The team needed it, so I built it.

---

### Hackathons: Two Wins

**Microsoft × Air India**
Partnered with Microsoft on an AI-powered solution to improve customer experience across Air India's touchpoints. Built and presented a working concept addressing real friction points passengers face—using AI to make the airline more responsive, more intuitive, more human.

**Internal: Time & Resource Platform**
Researched Firebase Studio, then designed and built an AI-powered internal platform for time tracking, resource allocation, and work management—in a single day. End-to-end: research, design, code, ship. Solved an operational pain point the organization hadn't prioritized.

Two different problems. Two different contexts. Same pattern: see a gap, build the solution, ship fast.

---

### What I Took From This

Two years inside a historic transformation taught me what most designers never experience: building infrastructure where none exists, shipping at startup speed inside enterprise constraints, designing for AI before the playbook existed, and creating culture alongside product.

The team I was part of went from inheriting nothing to winning Red Dot, Stevie Gold, Good Design Australia, and APEX recognition—in two years. That's not luck. That's what happens when you build from first principles under pressure.

I'm now looking for a remote role where this combination matters—product design with technical depth, AI-native thinking, and the ability to build from ambiguity.
